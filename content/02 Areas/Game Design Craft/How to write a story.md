# How to write a story

A method, not a story. Nothing here is about any particular project — it is the order to do things in and the tools to do them with.

Written for someone writing their first one.

## The single hardest idea

**A story is not a world, and it is not a sequence of events.**

Most first attempts are one of those two. A world is a place with lore and rules and history — interesting, and not a story. A sequence of events is "this happened, then this happened" — also not a story.

A story is: **somebody wants something, and has trouble getting it.** That is the whole definition. Everything below is detail on top of it.

If you have a brilliant world and no one who wants anything, you have no story yet. That is fine — you have the hard half done. But don't mistake it for the other half.

## 1 · Start with a want

Before anything else, fill in three blanks:

> **Who** wants **what**, but **what stops them?**

The want has to be concrete enough to fail at. "Wants to be happy" is not writable. "Wants to get home before dark" is.

The obstacle has to be real. If the character could just do the thing, there's no story — only an errand.

**Drill:** write five different versions of that sentence for the same character. Keep the one that makes you want to know what happens.

## 2 · Write the ending first

Counter-intuitive and it works. If you know where it lands, every scene has a direction to point.

You can change it later. Most people do. But writing toward *something* produces a draft; writing toward nothing produces a world.

**Drill:** one paragraph. What is the last thing that happens, and how is the character different from how they started?

## 3 · Something has to change

If the character is the same at the end, you wrote a situation.

The change does not have to be big and it does not have to be growth. They can learn something, lose something, give something up, or simply see the same thing differently. But something is different, and the story is the process of it becoming different.

**Ask of any draft:** what is different on the last page that wasn't true on the first? If the honest answer is "nothing", that's the problem, and it's usually the only problem.

## 4 · Connect scenes with "but" and "therefore"

The most useful practical tool there is.

Say your story out loud, one scene at a time. Between each pair of scenes, the word connecting them should be **but** or **therefore**. Never **and then**.

- *"He tries to get in, **but** the door is locked, **therefore** he climbs the wall, **but** someone sees him…"* — that's a story.
- *"He gets in, **and then** he finds a key, **and then** he walks to the tower…"* — that's a list.

"And then" means the scenes are in that order by accident. Either cut one or make one cause the other.

**Drill:** write your whole story as a chain of five sentences joined only by but/therefore. If you can't, you've found where it breaks.

## 5 · A scene is a unit of change

Each scene: somebody enters wanting something, and leaves in a different position. They got it, or they didn't, or they got it and it cost them.

If a scene ends where it started, it's a scene that exists to deliver information. Those are the first scenes to cut.

## 6 · Structures, and how to use them

Structures are **diagnostics, not recipes.** You write first, then hold a structure up against the draft to find where it sags. Writing to fill in a structure produces something that feels assembled.

| Structure | Shape | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **Three act** | Setup → things get worse → resolution | Conflict-driven stories. The default in western film |
| **Kishōtenketsu** | Introduce → develop → **twist** → reconcile | Stories without a villain. The twist reframes rather than escalates |
| **Five-act / Freytag** | Rise, climax, fall | Longer work, tragedy |
| **Hero's journey** | Call, threshold, ordeal, return | Useful as a *description* of stories that exist. A bad thing to write from — it produces the same story everyone has seen |

The twist in kishōtenketsu is the part people get wrong. It isn't a shock. It's a third element that makes you re-read the first two. → [[Kishotenketsu Level Structure]]

## 7 · Theme is discovered, not planned

Don't start with a message. Stories written to deliver a point read like homework, and the point is always the least interesting thing in them.

Write the story. Afterwards, notice what it keeps being about. *Then* you can sharpen it — strengthen what's already there rather than installing something that isn't.

## 8 · In a game, the player is the author of half of it

Everything above is true of novels and films. These are the parts that are only true of games.

The full process version of this section is [[How to write a game story]] — order of work, templates, and the production side. What follows is the short form.

**The player does things; the character can't contradict them.** If your story says the protagonist is reluctant and the player has spent an hour happily doing the thing, the story is wrong, not the player. Write around what the player actually does.

**The strongest beat is one the player performs.** A cutscene telling you something is the weakest version. A thing you do with your own hands is the strongest. Before writing a scene, ask whether it could be an action instead.

**You don't control pace.** A reader turns pages at your speed. A player might spend forty minutes in one room or sprint past your best moment. Anything that depends on timing needs to survive being ignored.

**You may not have words.** No dialogue, or no budget for it, means the story has to live in environment, behaviour, sound and consequence. That is a constraint, not a handicap — it's where games are better than film.

## 9 · The first draft is for you

Write it badly and fast. The first draft exists to find out what the story is; you cannot think your way to that in advance.

Then:

- **Cut the beginning.** Nearly every first draft starts too early. Find the first moment something actually happens and start there.
- **Cut anything that's only information.** If the reader doesn't need it to understand the next thing, it goes.
- **Read it out loud.** Every weak line announces itself.

## 10 · How to tell if it's working

Not "is it good". Too vague to act on.

**Tell it to someone in two minutes, out loud, without notes.** Watch their face. The place where they get bored is the place that's broken. The question they ask afterwards is the thing you failed to make clear — or the thing they're interested in, which may be more useful than what you planned.

For a game, the test is harder and better: **after playing, can they say what it was about?** Not recite the plot — say what it was *about*. If they can't, the story is in your document and not in the game.

## When you're stuck

| Stuck on | Try |
|---|---|
| Can't start | Write the ending. Or write the worst possible version, fast |
| It's boring | The character doesn't want anything badly enough. Raise what it costs to fail |
| Scenes feel random | Run the but/therefore test |
| Too many ideas | Cut to one character and one want. Add back only what that needs |
| It won't end | You have two stories. Find which want you actually care about |
| Nobody understood it | You explained the world and not the person |

## Worth reading

- **John Yorke, *Into the Woods*** — why story shapes are what they are. The best single book on structure
- **Ursula K. Le Guin, *Steering the Craft*** — sentence-level craft, short, exercises you can actually do
- **Robert McKee, *Story*** — the screenwriting standard. Dogmatic, and worth arguing with
- **Jonathan Blow, *The Main Conflicts in Modern Game Design*** — meaning that comes from mechanics rather than script → [[The Design Philosophy of Famous Game Designers  Sid Meier, Will Wright, Miyamoto and Kojima]]

For games specifically, three books, worked through in [[How to write a game story]]:

- **Heussner et al., *The Game Narrative Toolbox* (2nd ed.)** — the only one that is a process you can follow start to finish. Exercises at the end of every chapter
- **Bateman (ed.), *Game Writing: Narrative Skills for Videogames*** — the sharpest on theory. Narrative vs story, traits vs characteristics, the spine
- **Heussner (ed.), *The Advanced Game Narrative Toolbox*** — for later. Production, scheduling, research and consultants, adaptation
