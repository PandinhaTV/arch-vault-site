## 3 · After — close it, then write

### Claims worth keeping

- **Silence is a technique, not an absence.** Santaolalla scored a 15–25 hour game with 56 minutes of music, and stretches of over an hour pass with none. The silence is what lets you hear rain on a tin roof — and it is what makes the music mean something when it arrives. My problem was never the instruments; it was that something was always playing
- **One theme can carry an entire soundtrack.** Wintory wrote the Journey theme before development started, and that version appears nowhere in the game — every cue is a development of it. The theme stands for the player, so when it changes, you have changed
- **Deliberately placeless beats deliberately styled.** Wintory removed anything that read as a specific culture — percussion that sounded Arabic, a passage that went Irish — to keep it timeless. Taking things out was the work
- **Music can be an information device.** Mario Kart 8 tells you your race position, the stage of the race, and where you are, all through music — Nintendo will not let you turn it off, because it is part of how you read the game. The point generalises: music can carry information the HUD would otherwise have to
- **Recombinant cells.** Recombine pre-written motifs in real time with probabilities tied to gameplay parameters, across several layers at once. A cheap way to avoid hearing the same loop in a place players linger — the author's own technique on *Apotheon*, descended from Mozart's dice games

### Quotes

> I like space and silence. I don't want to manipulate the audience, I'd rather make it less obvious, I applied this to The Last of Us.
> — Gustavo Santaolalla, ch. 11

> I didn't want the music to feel ethnic or cultural in any way … I didn't make an effort to draw from anywhere, and in fact a few times the music accidentally sounded like it was from various cultures and so I would change to get rid of it.
> — Austin Wintory on Journey, ch. 10

### Disagreements

- **It is a book of case studies, so it has no method.** The preface admits this — *"not meant to provide a strict compositional framework"*. That is honest and it means the book cannot tell you what to do next; it can only show you what someone else did
- **Half the production chapters assume money.** The Cyberpunk chapter is partly about a £1,000 valve distortion unit. Interesting, useless. The composition techniques survive being poor; the production tools mostly do not
- **The case studies are AAA-shaped.** 20 iconic soundtracks means 20 budgets we do not have. Journey had a three-year composer engagement
- It leans on video examples hosted by the publisher, and the harmonic analysis is hard to follow without them
