# How I take notes from sources

A method for books, talks and videos. The template is [[Source Note]], and it is laid out in the order you fill it: **before**, **while**, **after**.

The guidance in the template is written in `%%` comments, so it is visible while you are editing and invisible when you read the note back. You never have to delete it.

Written 2026-08-19, when the method was new. Change it once you know what you actually do.

## The one rule

**A note is worth writing only if it changes something.** Not if it was interesting. Not if you might need it. If nothing in your work is different afterwards, you watched a video — which is fine, and does not need a note.

Everything below exists to make that more likely.

## Before — one minute

Write the question in **1 · Before** before you start.

Not "learn about X". Something you are actually stuck on. The difference:

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| I want to learn about design philosophy | Why does nobody who plays our game mention the story? |
| Learn about level design | Why did three testers get lost in a world made of glass? |

A weak question gives you a summary. A strong one gives you an answer, because you notice the parts that speak to it and skip the parts that don't.

You will not always have a strong question. Write the weak one anyway — it is still better than nothing, and you can sharpen it afterwards.

## During — write almost nothing

**Do not take notes while watching.** Notes taken during are transcription with extra steps; you end up copying phrasing you have not understood.

Capture only two things per moment, in the table under **2 · While**:

| Where | Reaction |
|---|---|
| 14:20 | this is our button problem |
| p. 230 | disagree |

That is the whole job.

## After — close it, then write

Close the book. Close the tab. **Then** write your claims, from memory.

This is the part that does the work. If you cannot state an idea without looking, you have not got it yet, and finding that out is the point. Whatever survives being recalled is what actually landed.

Then go back to your timestamps and fill in the quotes and details you wanted.

## How much

**Three to five claims. Hard cap.** The template gives you three bullets on purpose.

If everything seemed worth keeping, you were reading passively. Picking five forces the judgement that makes the note useful in six months. A note with twenty bullets is a transcript wearing a hat.

Same for quotes: one or two, and only if the exact phrasing matters. Otherwise put it in your own words as a claim.

## The two sections that get skipped

Both of your source notes so far have these empty, and they are the two that matter most. They sit under **3 · After**.

**Disagreements.** Where they are wrong, or where it does not apply to you. This is the difference between a note and a summary — anyone can summarise a video, only you can say why the advice does not fit a five-person team building a Frutiger Aero platformer. If you genuinely agree with everything, write *"nothing — but I was not looking for problems"* and move on. That is honest and still useful.

**Apply to.** One concrete thing, or an honest nothing. *"Interesting"* is not an application. *"Try Ueda's subtraction rule on the September cut list"* is.

Both can be short. Neither should be blank without a reason.

## What to do with transcripts

A pasted transcript feels like capture. It is not — it is the source, moved. It makes the note *look* full while the thinking has not happened.

Keep it if it is the only record, but fold it into a collapsed callout at the bottom so it never competes with your own words:

```markdown
> [!note]- Transcript
> ...
```

## The follow-up

Every source names other sources. Put them in **Follow-ups** as you go — that is how one video becomes a reading list instead of a dead end.

## When to skip the note entirely

- You watched it for enjoyment
- You already knew all of it
- You cannot name one thing you would do differently

Deleting a note you started is allowed. A vault full of notes you never reopen is worse than a small one you trust.

## The check that keeps this honest

The template ends with **Verdict** — *did this change anything?* Answer it when you close the note, not months later. "No" is a real answer, and it tells you something about how you chose the source.

Once a month, open `03 Resources/Learning` and read the verdicts. A run of "no" means the problem is upstream: you are picking sources by what looks interesting rather than by what you are stuck on.
