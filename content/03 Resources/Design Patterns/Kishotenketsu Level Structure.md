# Kishōtenketsu Level Structure

The four-part structure the team used to divide [[Aquatic]], and partly [[Office]].

![[diagram-kishotenketsu.svg]]

## The four parts

**Ki** — introduce the idea. Show the mechanic in its simplest form.
**Shō** — develop it. The same idea, more demanding.
**Ten** — twist. Show the idea from a new angle.
**Ketsu** — conclude. Combine everything, with no new material.

## Why it suits platformers

The structure has no conflict in it. Western three-act structure escalates through opposition — the obstacle gets harder, the enemy gets stronger. Kishōtenketsu develops by reframing instead.

For a game about movement rather than combat, that works better. The twist is not a bigger threat. It is the player realising the grapple can be used in a way they had not considered.

Nintendo's 2D Mario levels use this structure, which is part of why they teach without tutorials.

## How to check the twist works

A good Ten makes the player think "I can do that?" rather than "this is harder now".

The common failure is building Ki, Shō and Ketsu, then making the third section simply harder and calling it the twist. That produces a level that escalates without surprising anyone.

Worth checking here, because [[Aquatic]]'s twist section is not identified anywhere, and [[Office]]'s map only labels Ki and Shō.

## In this project

The aquatic level's sections are explicitly divided by kishōtenketsu, per §4.1.3.

The final tower's map is annotated with Ki and Shō only. Either Ten and Ketsu are unbuilt, or the boss fight is meant to be both.
